# R1 — Pain points: who has a will or trust, who doesn't, why, what makes people act, and what happens without a plan

Prepared for: Carbon Law Group, "Aprende y Planea" (research only; no repository files changed).
Date: 2026-11-18. Method: web search plus reading the primary pages and PDFs listed in Sources. Every number below was seen in the cited source unless it is marked **second-hand**. Sources from before 2019 are marked **(older)**.

---

## Summary

- **Most U.S. adults have no will, and Latinos have the fewest.** Recent national rates range from 24% (Caring.com/YouGov 2025) to 32% (Pew 2025) to 46% (Gallup 2021). The spread comes from different survey methods. Every source that splits by race or ethnicity puts Hispanic adults at or near the bottom. Caring.com 2025 found 14% of Hispanic respondents with a will vs. 28% of White respondents. Urban Institute (HRS 2020) found **76% of Hispanic homeowners over 50 have no will or trust**, vs. 35% of White homeowners.
- **The gap is not only about money.** In HRS data, Hispanic respondents were about 18.5 percentage points less likely to have a will than non-Hispanic Whites, even with homeownership, wealth, education and other factors held constant (Boston College CRR 2023).
- **The top reason is "I haven't gotten around to it."** It is the leading answer in every survey that asks: 43% (Caring 2024 and 2025), 61% of adults 50+ (AARP 2024), and the top reason in CRR/NORC 2023. Next come "I don't have enough to leave" (40%, Caring 2024; 21%, AARP), "I don't know how to start" (19%, AARP), "I don't want to think about it" (16%, AARP; 41% for written health-care wishes in California, CHCF 2019), and cost (15%, AARP; most often cited by people earning under $40k, Caring 2024).
- **Latino-specific barriers are documented mostly in qualitative work.** HAC and UnidosUS listening sessions (2026 report) name verbal agreements and informal succession, reluctance to talk about death, mixed immigration status, language, distrust of the legal system, and multigenerational households. Language has a measured effect for health-care directives. In a Northern California health system (n = 620,948), 10.9% of Hispanic patients 55+ had an advance directive vs. 20.3% of non-Hispanic Whites. Spanish speakers who needed an interpreter were the least likely to have one (Gonzalez et al. 2021).
- **Fear of immigration enforcement is high and rising.** In late 2025, 52% of U.S. Latinos (Pew, n = 4,923) and 53% of Hispanic immigrants (KFF/NYT, n = 1,805) worried that they or someone close to them could be deported. 43% of immigrants worried about being separated from their children or family. In California, 20% of children live in mixed-status families. A plan for who cares for the children is an urgent, concrete need for these families. California's AB 495 (signed Oct. 2025) adds new tools for it.
- **The things that move people are health, children, deaths in the family and property.** A medical diagnosis is the motivator people without a will name most often (37%, Caring 2025). Having a child was the top real-life motivator for people who did make a will (CRR/NORC 2023). For Hispanic respondents in 2025, family growth (27%), employer benefits (22%) and national events (22%) all rose (Caring 2025).
- **Setting matters more than cash.** In a NORC experiment, offering a will during a mortgage closing *lowered* stated intent. A $500 incentive moved only White and "financially sophisticated" respondents. A calmer setting (opening a bank account) moved non-White respondents (CRR 2023). Inference: Aprende's calm, step-by-step format fits this better than the $100 discount alone.
- **Without a plan, California families face court time, fees taken from gross value, divided homes and court-chosen guardians.** Formal probate typically takes 9–18 months (California Courts). Statutory attorney fees are based on the estate's gross appraised value "without reference to encumbrances" (Prob. Code §10810), and the personal representative can claim the same again. In San Francisco about half of probate filings each year are intestate. Most older homeowners without a will have several children, so the house is split among co-heirs, which can produce "heirs' property" (Urban 2024; HAC/UnidosUS 2026). One correction for the lessons: since April 2025, a primary residence worth $750,000 or less can pass by a simpler court petition instead of formal probate (AB 2016 / Prob. Code §13151, **second-hand**).

## Findings

### F1. Who has a will or trust: national rates (they conflict)

| Source (who ran it, year, sample) | Will | Other |
|---|---|---|
| Caring.com + YouGov, 2025 Wills & Estate Planning Study, n = 2,541 adults (online panel) | **24%** | 13% living trust; 4% other documents; down from 33% in 2022 |
| Caring.com + YouGov, 2024 study, n = 2,400+ (2,481 per the 2025 race page) | **32%** | 64% say a will is very/somewhat important |
| Pew Research Center, American Trends Panel, Sept. 2–8, 2025, n = 8,750 | **32%** | 31% living will / advance health care directive; 20% funeral arrangements |
| CRR at Boston College + NORC AmeriSpeak, April 2023, n = 3,047 adults 25+ | **34%** | 66% have no will |
| Gallup phone poll, May 3–18, 2021, n = 1,016 | **46%** | Readings since 1990 range from 44% to 51%; 45% had a living will (Gallup 2020) |
| AARP Research, April 2024, n = 1,869 adults 50+ (oversamples of Hispanic, Black, AANHPI) | **51%** of 50+ | 38% at 50–64, 66% at 65+; 93% say having one is important |

**Conflict to note.** Gallup's phone poll runs well above Caring's opt-in online panel. Pew's probability panel (32%, 2025) falls in between and is the most rigorous recent national figure. There is also an internal conflict in Caring's data. The 2024 study reports 32% overall, but the 2025 race-and-gender page reports 2024 subgroup rates of only 14.6%–27.2%. The race page probably uses a narrower category, such as "will only" (inference; Caring does not say). For trends, use one survey series at a time.

### F2. Who doesn't have one: age, income, education

- **Age.** Gallup 2021: 20% of adults 18–29 have a will, 36% at 30–49, 53% at 50–64, 76% at 65+. Pew 2025: most Americans have no will until their 70s; 66% in their 70s and about 80% at 80+ have one; 46% in their 60s; about a third or fewer under 60.
- **Parents of minors.** Caring 2025: "Americans with children under 18 comprise the largest cohort of people without wills or other estate planning documents." The 35–54 group is the largest group without documents.
- **Income.** Gallup 2021: 30% of households under $40k have a will vs. 61% at $100k+. Pew 2025, adults 70+: 83% of upper-income vs. 51% of lower-income have a will. Caring 2024: 21% of people earning $40k or less, down from 25% in 2020.
- **Education.** Caring 2024: more than half of people with a post-graduate degree have a will vs. 23% of people with high school or less.

### F3. Race and ethnicity, with a focus on Hispanic/Latino households

- **Caring.com 2025 (YouGov, n = 2,541; 16% of respondents Hispanic):** 14% of Hispanic respondents had a will in 2025 (14.6% in 2024), vs. 28% White, 16% Black, 23% "Other." More than half of every racial and gender group had no estate planning documents at all. Caring 2024 says estate planning among Hispanic Americans fell 21% since 2020 (a relative change).
- **Gallup 2021:** 55% of White adults vs. 28% of nonwhite adults have a will. Gallup does not split Hispanic adults out.
- **Urban Institute (Zinn, Neal & Walsh, Oct. 2024; Health and Retirement Study 2020):** 76% of Hispanic homeowners over 50 and nearly 70% of Black homeowners have no will or trust, vs. 35% of White homeowners (42.4% of all owners over 50). More than 60% of Black and Hispanic homeowners without a will have less than $500 in liquid assets, vs. 27% of White homeowners. NAHREP's 2025 State of Hispanic Wealth Report repeats this as 24% of Hispanic homeowners over 50 having a will or trust, "the lowest rate of any racial or ethnic demographic."
- **CRR, "Wills, Wealth, and Race" (Aubry, Munnell & Wettstein, WP 2023-10; HRS 1992–2018):** In the regression on having a will, the Hispanic coefficient is −0.185 (about 18.5 percentage points lower than non-Hispanic Whites). The model controls for homeownership, non-housing wealth, education and other factors. Hispanic respondents were also much less likely to have received an inheritance (−0.297 raw). The companion brief (IB 24-9) states that among households 70+ in 2020, the White share with a will was "more than twice" the Black and Hispanic shares.
- **AARP 2024:** White and AANHPI adults 50+ are more likely than Black and Hispanic adults to have a will. The public summary gives no Hispanic percentage. 14% of Hispanic adults 50+ had received a substantial inheritance or gift, vs. 28% of White adults. People whose parents had a will are more likely to have one.
- **Hispanic Wealth Project / NAHREP:** The ten-year goal is to raise the share of Hispanic households with a will or trust to 30% or more (2024 and 2025 reports). A Hispanic Wealth Project social post says trust ownership among Latinos rose from 7.0% (2024) to 11.1% (2025) in the 2026 report. **Second-hand:** I saw this only in a search snippet of that post, not in the report itself.
- **Spanish-speaking households (health-care directives):** Gonzalez et al., *J Am Geriatr Soc* 2021. Retrospective records review in a Northern California integrated health system, 620,948 patients 55+, 2013–2017. 20.3% of non-Hispanic Whites vs. 10.9% of Hispanics had an advance directive. Among Hispanic patients, English speakers had adjusted odds of 2.6 and Spanish speakers who did not need an interpreter had 1.2, compared with Spanish speakers who needed one.
- **California statewide (health-care wishes in writing):** CHCF "Help Wanted," PerryUndem, 2019, n = 2,588 adult Californians. 34% have their medical wishes in writing (up from 23% in 2011). Latino respondents and people under 150% of the poverty line were the most likely to say they do not.
- **San Francisco (2026 local estimate):** The Board of Supervisors' Budget & Legislative Analyst (Oct. 6, 2026) estimates that 58,295–77,726 of the city's 194,316 adults 60+ have no will. It notes that people who are lower-income, non-white and less educated are less likely to have these documents. 36% of the city's 60+ population speaks English less than "very well."
- **Language base:** Pew 2023 (n = 3,029 Latino adults, Aug. 2022, English and Spanish) found 75% of U.S. Latinos can carry on a conversation in Spanish pretty well or very well, and close to 40 million speak Spanish at home.

**Gap in the evidence:** I found no survey that measures will or trust ownership separately for Spanish-dominant, immigrant, or mixed-status households, or for Los Angeles County. The Federal Reserve SCF is used by researchers (for example, Bea & Taylor Poppe) but publishes no will tables in its public reports. The UnidosUS–HAC report also says data is limited.

### F4. Immigrants and mixed-status families

- **Pew National Survey of Latinos, Oct. 6–16, 2025, n = 4,923, bilingual:** 52% of Latinos worry that they, a family member or a close friend could be deported (42% in March 2025; 44% in 2019). Pew estimates 18% of Latino adults were unauthorized in 2023. 59% had seen or heard of ICE arrests or raids in their local area.
- **KFF/New York Times 2025 Survey of Immigrants, Aug. 28–Oct. 20, 2025, n = 1,805 (431 completed in Spanish):** 41% of immigrants worry that they or a family member could be detained or deported (26% in 2023). The share is 53% of Hispanic immigrants, 75% of likely undocumented immigrants, and 50% of lawfully present immigrants. 43% worry about being separated from children or family. 60% of immigrant parents worry about at least one immigration-related outcome.
- **California Immigrant Data Portal (USC Equity Research Institute), 2023 data:** 20% of all Californians under 18 live in mixed-status families. More than 3.3 million U.S. citizens and lawful residents live in mixed-status families.
- **HAC & UnidosUS, "Heirs' Property in Latino Communities" (June 2026; 2025 listening sessions):** Mixed status "can limit access to legal aid and recourse." Some attorneys were uncertain which cases they could take. Listening sessions also named language barriers and "distrust or unfamiliarity with formal legal systems." One partner organization uses a "Readiness Packet" for deportation or detention crises.
- **California law responses:**
  - The **Caregiver's Authorization Affidavit** needs no court filing and no notary (California Courts self-help).
  - **AB 495, the Family Preparedness Plan Act of 2025**, was signed Oct. 12, 2025 (Alliance for Children's Rights). It lets parents petition for joint guardianship in anticipation of deportation without ending their parental rights. It also expands "relative" for the affidavit to kin within the fifth degree (Youth Law Center fact sheet, 2026).
  - **Prob. Code §1500** lets a parent nominate a guardian for a minor child.
- **Fraud risk:** Public Counsel (2026) calls notario fraud "constant and pervasive" in California. It cites a study from nearly 30 years ago **(older)**: 71% of undocumented immigrants in L.A. County who got legal advice went to a notario. In a national survey of immigration practitioners, more than 40% said clients did not report fraud for fear of their immigration status. California law bars translating "notary public" as "notario público" (Bus. & Prof. Code §22442.3; seen via a FindLaw snippet, **second-hand**).

### F5. Why people don't make one

| Reason | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Procrastination / low priority | 43% "just haven't gotten around to it" (Caring 2024 and 2025). 61% of adults 50+ (AARP 2024). Top reason in CRR/NORC 2023. Caring 2025: 56% say it is low on the to-do list or they don't have enough assets. 42% "too busy" for written health-care wishes (CHCF 2019, California). |
| "I don't have enough" | 40% in 2024, up from 33% in 2022 (Caring 2024). 49% of people earning $40k or less vs. 25% at $80k+ (Caring 2024). 21% of adults 50+ (AARP). NAHREP calls this a "myth" for Hispanic households, which control $8 trillion in assets, including $4.2 trillion in real estate (SHWR 2024). |
| Don't know how / process feels confusing | 19% "don't know how to get started" (AARP). 34% "don't know how to put wishes in a legal document" (CHCF 2019). CRR: many answers "suggested that people were generally baffled by the process." Caring 2025: 16% of people without a will had started researching online and 20% had talked to a loved one, which suggests they want to start but don't know how. |
| Not wanting to think about death | 16% (AARP). 41% for written medical wishes (CHCF 2019). HAC/UnidosUS: families "may be reluctant to discuss death and inheritance." I found no peer-reviewed measure of "superstition/tempting fate"; law-firm blogs mention it, but that is anecdote, not evidence. |
| Cost | 15% "can't afford" (AARP). Lowest-income group most likely to call a will too expensive (Caring 2024). LSC Justice Gap 2022 (NORC, >5,000 adults): 46% of low-income people who didn't seek legal help cite cost, and 53% don't know whether they could find and afford a lawyer. Urban: a will can cost $500–$1,500, and most Black and Hispanic older homeowners without one have under $500 liquid. |
| Distrust / unfamiliarity with lawyers and courts | LSC 2022: 39% of low-income vs. 59% of higher-income people believe they can use the civil legal system to protect their rights. Urban 2024 and HAC/UnidosUS 2026 cite discomfort with and distrust of the legal system (qualitative). |
| "I trust my family" / informal arrangements | 47% "I trust my family to make decisions for me," the top reason for no written health wishes (CHCF 2019). HAC/UnidosUS: verbal agreements and informal succession in Latino families. CRR/NORC: the second reason for no will is "I have named beneficiaries" on accounts. |
| Language | Strong evidence for health-care directives only (Gonzalez 2021). For wills, it is named in NAHREP 2025 and HAC/UnidosUS 2026, but not measured. |
| Immigration fear | Widespread worry (F4). Its effect on will-making has not been measured. HAC/UnidosUS: mixed status limits access to legal aid. |
| Family conflict | No good U.S. survey found that measures this as a reason for not making a will. Urban 2024 and CRR note that co-heirs who disagree or live in the house cause problems after death (see F7). |

### F6. What finally makes people do it

- **Health.** A medical diagnosis or health concern is the top motivator named by people without a plan: 37% in 2025, 43% in 2024 (Caring). Lower-income people were 60% more likely than the highest earners to cite a diagnosis as the reason they made a will (Caring 2024).
- **Children.** Having a child was the top motivator for people who did write a will. Next were the death of someone close and a recommendation from parents, family or friends (CRR/NORC 2023). Family expansion was the third most common reason in Caring 2025; among Hispanic respondents it rose from 23% to 27%.
- **Deaths in the family.** This was the second most common reason in Caring 2025 (26%, up from 22%), but it did not rise among Hispanic respondents.
- **Home and assets.** In 2025, 15% of Hispanic respondents (down from 20%) cited buying a home or other asset (Caring race page). Buying property is also a top reason for updating a will (30% of updates involved a raise, an inheritance or a property purchase).
- **Retirement or an age milestone.** This was the top reason across every gender and racial group in Caring 2024–2025.
- **Employer benefits.** Among Hispanic respondents this rose from 13% to 22% in 2025, the largest increase of any group (Caring race page).
- **National and world events.** Among Hispanic respondents this rose from 14% to 22% (Caring race page). In the overall sample only 8% named current events (Caring 2025).
- **Parents' example.** People whose parents had a will are more likely to have one (AARP 2024).
- **Setting (experiment).** CRR/NORC 2023 tested settings with people who had no will. Linking a will to a mortgage cut stated intent from 79.9% to 71.0%. A $500 add-on recovered only half of that drop and worked mainly for White and "sophisticated" respondents. Moving the offer to a bank-account opening raised intent by 9.8 points relative to the mortgage offer, and the effect was significant only for non-White respondents.
- **A floor.** About a quarter of people say nothing would motivate them: 23% in 2024, 24% in 2023 (Caring).

### F7. What happens to families without a plan (California focus)

- **Intestacy.** California's default rules (Prob. Code §6401):
  - The surviving spouse takes the decedent's half of community property.
  - For separate property, the spouse takes all, one-half, or one-third, depending on children, parents and siblings. The spouse takes one-third if there is more than one child, and the rest goes to the children.
  - Researchers note intestacy rules count only legally recognized kin and follow degree of relationship (CRR WP 2023-10, describing Bea & Taylor Poppe 2021). A non-resident child outranks a resident grandchild, and minors can inherit outright.
  - Using the Federal Reserve SCF, Bea & Taylor Poppe (*Law & Society Review*, 2021) estimate more than 21% of U.S. families have a structure that intestacy rules "marginalize." The risk is higher for Black and Hispanic households. **Second-hand:** from the abstract snippet and the CRR summary; the full article was blocked.
- **Probate time and cost.**
  - Formal probate "typically takes 9 to 18 months and can sometimes take even longer" (California Courts Self-Help Guide).
  - Attorney fees for ordinary services are 4% of the first $100k, 3% of the next $100k and 2% of the next $800k, based on appraised value "without reference to encumbrances" (Prob. Code §10810, read on leginfo). The personal representative's compensation uses the same schedule (Prob. Code §10800, per SF BLA 2026).
  - Worked example (my calculation from §10810): a $900,000 gross estate yields $21,000 in attorney fees plus $21,000 to the personal representative, even if the house has a large mortgage. SF BLA computes $26,213 + $26,213 = $52,426 for San Francisco's median older-adult home value of $1,321,300.
  - Urban cites probate costs of 3–10% of an estate's value (**second-hand** within Urban).
- **Simpler path for modest homes (important for accuracy).** For deaths on or after April 1, 2025, a California primary residence with gross value of $750,000 or less can pass by a court petition (form DE-310) instead of formal probate (AB 2016; Prob. Code §13151). Sources: Best Best & Krieger legal alert, Aug. 2026; Sacramento County Public Law Library. **Second-hand:** leginfo blocked automated access to §13151. The petition is still a court process, and other assets are not covered by it.
- **How common intestate probate is.** San Francisco Superior Court, 2020–2025: 355–492 probate estate filings a year, of which 169–240 were intestate and 155–192 testate (SF BLA 2026).
- **Divided homes / heirs' property.**
  - Most older homeowners without wills have several children, so the property is split into fractional shares (Urban 2024, HRS 2020).
  - Heir-owners without clear title can be shut out of many loans, repair financing, tax relief and disaster aid, and face partition or forced sale (Urban 2024; HAC/UnidosUS 2026).
  - HAC conservatively identified about 14,100 likely or at-risk heirs' properties in Latino-majority census tracts across 41 states and territories (about 0.2% of parcels). HAC says this is an "extremely conservative estimate."
  - Title problems often surface only in a crisis (foreclosure, disaster, urgent repair) (HAC/UnidosUS 2026).
- **Lost bequests.** In HRS exit interviews, decedents with a will were more likely to leave the bequests they expected. Hispanic decedents were more likely to fall short of a $100,000 target (CRR WP 2023-10).
- **Minor children.** Without a nomination, a court decides who becomes guardian. A guardianship puts the court in a supervising role until the child turns 18 (California Courts). A parent can nominate a guardian (Prob. Code §1500). Non-court options include the Caregiver's Authorization Affidavit, and joint guardianship under AB 495 (F4).
- **Disputes and burden.** CRR and Urban describe co-heirs who disagree, or who live in the house, as a common source of problems (descriptive, not measured). CHCF 2019: Californians' top end-of-life priority is that their families not be burdened, by costs (70% "extremely important"), by tough decisions (60%) or by caregiving (59%).
- **Reverse transfers.** 16% of Hispanic older adults received more than $500 from their adult children in the two years before death, vs. 13% overall (Urban, Zhong 2026, HRS 2010–22). About 80% of parents leave each child the same amount.

## Implications for Aprende

Each item names the app part, the finding it rests on, and a confidence level. "Inference" means the evidence does not test this directly.

### Lesson content
1. **Make "Yo no tengo suficiente" an early, named myth.** Show that a modest house is enough to need a plan, and that probate fees are based on gross value, not equity. *Rests on F5 ("I don't have enough," 40%; higher in low-income groups), F7 (§10810).* **Confidence: high.**
2. **Correct the probate scare for modest homes.** Do not imply every house goes through 9–18 months of formal probate. State that a home of $750,000 or less now has a simpler court petition. Then explain what a will or trust still adds: who gets it, who is in charge, guardians, no splitting by default, no court for a trust. The probate bonus lesson should cite §13151, and the attorney should confirm this. *Rests on F7.* **Confidence: high** that the content needs it. The law should be re-checked on leginfo because I read it second-hand.
3. **Teach "beneficiary forms are not a whole plan."** Many people think account beneficiaries cover them. Only 65% of adults 50+ have named beneficiaries on all accounts. *Rests on F5 (CRR second reason), AARP 2024.* **Confidence: medium.**
4. **Add a family-preparedness bonus lesson for parents.** Cover nominating a guardian in the will (§1500), the Caregiver's Authorization Affidavit, and AB 495 joint guardianship. Frame it as general information, and route the person's own situation to the attorney. It may fall outside the firm's core documents, so this is a firm decision (see the firm-workflow section). *Rests on F4, F6 (children are the top real-life motivator), F2 (parents of minors are the largest group without documents).* **Confidence: high** on need, **medium** on scope fit.
5. **Explain intestacy with the fictional Torres family.** Use cases that hit Latino family structures: an unmarried partner, a grandchild being raised at home, an adult child living in the house, a child born outside the U.S. *Rests on F7 (Bea & Taylor Poppe; §6401).* **Confidence: medium** (the intestacy rule is high; the Latino prevalence is inference).
6. **Make the health care directive lesson especially strong in Spanish.** The gap between people who need an interpreter and English speakers is the best-measured language effect. *Rests on F3 (Gonzalez 2021).* **Confidence: high.**

### Intake questions ("Tus datos")
7. **Ask how the house is titled, who lives in it, and about any verbal promises** ("¿Le prometió la casa a alguien?"). Also ask about property outside California or the U.S. *Rests on F5/F7 (verbal agreements, heirs' property).* **Confidence: medium.** The property-abroad question is inference.
8. **Ask about family structure without assuming marriage.** Ask about unmarried partners, children of other relationships, stepchildren, and grandchildren being raised in the home. *Rests on F7 (marginalized family forms).* **Confidence: medium.**
9. **Do not ask for immigration status unless the attorney decides it is needed.** Ask about guardian and backup choices with no status question. Let the attorney decide what else to ask. *Rests on F4 (fear; status limits legal access; fraud reports suppressed by fear).* **Confidence: medium** (inference from fear data). The firm must decide.
10. **Ask the "trigger" questions as gentle context, not as pressure.** Examples: a recent diagnosis, a new baby, a recent death, a home purchase. Use the answers to order what the attorney reviews. *Rests on F6.* **Confidence: medium.**

### Character tone
11. **Frame lessons around protecting family and the house, not around death.** "Que su familia no cargue con decisiones ni gastos" matches Californians' top end-of-life priority. El Oso's calm mode fits the hard topics. *Rests on F7 (CHCF 70%/60%), F5 (death avoidance 16–41%; reluctance in Latino families).* **Confidence: high.**
12. **Speak to "I trust my family" directly.** Say that a plan is how you make sure the family can act, and that it does not replace trusting them. *Rests on F5 (CHCF 47%).* **Confidence: medium.**
13. **Use "your parents planned / plan for your kids" stories.** People whose parents had wills are more likely to have one. Advice from family is the third motivator. This suits Marco (the son) as a messenger. *Rests on F3 (AARP), F6 (CRR).* **Confidence: medium.**

### Timeline and reminders
14. **The 14-day plan already answers the #1 barrier (procrastination).** Use reminders that name the next small step, not deadlines. Keep "no countdown timers." *Rests on F5 (43–61%), F6 (CRR: pressured settings reduce intent).* **Confidence: high.**
15. **Expect a group that will not move. Do not chase them.** Offer a learning-only path and one gentle check-in. *Rests on F6 (23–24% say "nothing").* **Confidence: medium.**
16. **Time outreach to life events if the firm markets** (births, home purchases, diagnoses via community or health partners, employer benefits). The employer-benefit trigger rose most for Hispanic respondents. *Rests on F6.* **Confidence: medium.**

### Pricing and discounts
17. **Do not count on the $100-per-lesson discount alone to persuade.** In the CRR experiment, $500 did not move non-White or less financially sophisticated respondents; a calmer setting did. The discount still has value as a fair reward and a clear price. *Rests on F6.* **Confidence: medium.** This is a hypothetical-intent experiment, not real behavior.
18. **Offer a payment plan or split payment, and show the final price early.** More than 60% of older Hispanic homeowners without a will have under $500 liquid. Cost is a stated barrier for the lowest-income group. *Rests on F3/F5 (Urban; Caring 2024; LSC).* **Confidence: high** on the need. The design is a firm decision.

### Trust and safety copy
19. **Say clearly that this is a licensed California attorney, not a notario.** Show the attorney's State Bar number and a link to verify it, and say that a notary public cannot give legal advice. *Rests on F4 (notario fraud in L.A.), F5 (distrust).* **Confidence: high.**
20. **Write a plain-Spanish data promise about who can see the client's information.** Only make the promise the firm can keep: what is stored, for how long, and that it is protected by attorney-client confidentiality. The firm must confirm the exact wording. *Rests on F4 (52–53% worry; fear suppresses fraud reports).* **Confidence: medium** (inference from fear data).
21. **Make the person feel the human is real.** Show the attorney's photo and name, and that the attorney reviews everything. This answers both distrust and the AI disclosure. *Rests on F5 (LSC: 39% of low-income people believe the system protects them; HAC distrust).* **Confidence: medium.**

### Firm workflow
22. **Plan for Spanish at the signing.** Provide Spanish-speaking staff or a qualified interpreter, Spanish summaries of each document, and witnesses who understand the client. Interpreter-dependent clients are the least likely to complete directives. *Rests on F3 (Gonzalez).* **Confidence: high.**
23. **Prepare for the adult-child helper (Marco).** Meet the client alone for part of the review, because adult children help with forms in multigenerational households. *Rests on F5/F7 (multigenerational households, informal succession).* This is legal-practice inference, not survey evidence. **Confidence: medium.**
24. **Decide the scope for family-preparedness and guardianship-only clients.** Also decide whether to keep a referral list for immigration counsel and legal aid that serves mixed-status families. *Rests on F4 (HAC: few providers are equipped for mixed-status families).* **Confidence: medium.**
25. **Screen for title problems and refer them.** If intake shows an inherited home still in a dead parent's name, flag it for the attorney. *Rests on F7 (heirs' property surfaces in crisis).* **Confidence: medium.**
